The first post in this series went out on 19 October 2026. This is the hundred and third. The numbers below come from Search Console, the cookie-free analytics on this site, and the contact form's own records, and they are the honest version, including the posts that did nothing.

## The numbers

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Posts published | 52 | 52 |
| Search impressions per month, end of year | ~41,000 | ~118,000 |
| Search clicks per month, end of year | ~1,900 | ~6,400 |
| Posts in the top 10 for at least one tracked query | 14 | 39 |
| Enquiries that mentioned a post | 6 | 17 |
| Enquiries that became paid work | 2 | 5 |

Seven projects over two years, from a blog that costs a few hours a week. That is the whole business case.

## What ranks

The posts in the top ten share a shape: a specific problem, a real example, the actual code or config, and a sources list. The ones that rank best are the ones where the title is the search:

- "Deploying a Node.js app on a Linux VPS with Nginx, PM2 and SSL": the first post, still the most-read, page one for several deployment queries.
- "Fixing slow MongoDB queries in a Node API": page one for a handful of explain-plan queries.
- "Gmail app passwords with Nodemailer, honestly": ranks because it says what breaks, and the comments on other sites' tutorials are full of people hitting exactly that.
- The Capacitor posts: a small niche with little good writing in it, so even modest posts rank.
- "Indian number and date formatting in JavaScript": the lakh-grouping question has a one-line answer and no one else had written it plainly.

## What brought work

Not the same list. Enquiries came from posts that describe a *business* problem a reader recognises:

- The HRMS schema and attendance posts: three enquiries from companies with attendance pain.
- The CRM lead-desk post: two enquiries, both small firms losing enquiries in WhatsApp.
- The SEBI compliance post: one enquiry from a broker, which became the largest project of the second year.
- The estimation and contract posts: no direct enquiries, but mentioned in three first calls as the reason the person felt comfortable reaching out.

The technical deep-dives build trust; the business-problem posts start conversations. Both are needed and they are different posts.

## What did nothing

- The Three.js product hero post: well-read for a week after a LinkedIn share, then nothing. No search demand for the problem as I framed it.
- The "taking over a PHP codebase" post: ranks for nothing, read by nobody. The audience for it does not search; they inherit a codebase and suffer.
- Three posts that are too similar to earlier ones. Search engines picked one and ignored the others. Year two has fewer of these because I started checking the existing list before writing.

## How long ranking takes

A new post on this domain now appears in results within a week and settles into its position in two to four months. In the first six months of the blog, the same process took four to eight months. The domain's overall standing is what changed, and it is built by the whole body of posts, not any one.

## The change that mattered most

Moving from the Vercel subdomain to a custom domain, in the second year, and setting up the redirects properly. Within three months impressions were up by more than the previous six months of posts had achieved. The post about that move is in this series. If I were starting again, the custom domain would be day one.

## Second: internal links

In the second year every post links to two or three earlier posts where the reader would benefit, and the older posts were revisited to link forward. Posts that had been flat for months moved up after being linked from newer ones. This took an afternoon per quarter.

## What I would not write again

Anything whose title I would not type into a search box myself. Anything I had not actually done. Anything that could be about any project. The posts that work are specific, done, and mine; that filter would have removed about ten of the hundred and four, and the blog would be better for it.

## What continues

The schedule stays weekly. The topics for the next year are already listed, and about a third are updates to posts in this series as the tools change, which is the maintenance a body of technical writing needs to keep being true.
